How would you grow a motorcycle brand that's about to shut down?
This is a real BCG interview question.
Your client is a 100-year-old motorcycle company.
Its factory can make 6,000 bikes a month, but it's selling only 2,000.
The board is thinking about shutting it down.
The CEO asks you: how do we grow it?
This is how you would solve it, consultant style.
Before jumping to an answer, I asked a few clarifying questions, such as:
Why aren't people buying from us?
She said customers think that our bikes are heavy, old-fashioned and unreliable, and Japanese commuter bikes are cheaper and give better mileage.
Next question is: who still buys from us?
She said a small group of loyal fans, mostly older men, who love the bike's thump and its history.
Then I asked: how do we sell today?
She said through 200 dealers, each selling about 10 bikes a month.
And when someone does take a test ride, 1 in 5 end up buying.
The problem is very few people ever take one.
So how do we grow it?
We need to 3x our sales, so there are 4 ways to grow any brand:
the same product to the same customers, the same product to new customers, new products for the same customers, new products for new customers.
That's also called, by the way, the Ansoff matrix.
First: same bike, same customers.
Our fans are our best salespeople, so turn them into a channel.
Every dealer runs a monthly group ride, and every owner who brings in a buyer gets a free service.
If each of our 200 dealers sells about 5 more bikes a month this way, that's about 1,000 more bikes.
Second: same bike, but new customers.
People who ride this bike love it.
The problem is that too few people ever ride it, so we need to get a lot more people on it for the first time, that is, increase our trials.
Put rental bikes in Goa, Manali and Leh, so their first try happens on holiday, and offer doorstep test drives booked online.
Since 1 in 5 test riders buy today, every 5,000 extra first rides a month brings in a thousand more bikes.
Third: new products for the same customers.
Why don't people buy? Because it's heavy, it breaks down.
So fix exactly that, fix the reliability, and make a lighter version, but keep the thump, the shape and the history, because that's the reason anyone buys it at all, so we don't want to let go of the legacy.
Fourth: new products for new customers.
Sell accessories like riding jackets, helmets, saddlebags and custom parts through dealers and online.
Every bike already on the road becomes a customer again.
So, I'd start with the first two, because they don't need a new factory or a new product.
I'd fix the bike in parallel and add accessories in the existing dealers once the brand is growing again.
